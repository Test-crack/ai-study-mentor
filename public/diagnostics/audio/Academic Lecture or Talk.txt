Instead, we are deeply
influenced by the way
information is presented to us,

example. Imagine you're told
that a new medical treatment has
a 90% support survival rate.
Most people would support it.
But if you're told the same
treatment has a 10% mortality
rate, people are far less
enthusiastic. Even though
mathematically these two
statements are identical, the
framing survival versus
mortality profoundly affects our
perception of risk. This led
Kahneman and Tversky to develop
what became known as prospect
theory, published in 1979. The
central insight is people do not
evaluate outcomes in absolute
terms. Instead, we assess them
relative to a reference point,
usually our current situation or
what we expected to happen.
Gains and losses are treated
asymmetrically. Losing $100
feels roughly twice as painful
as gaining $100 feels
pleasurable. This phenomenon is
called loss aversion, and it has
enormous implications for
everything from consumer
behavior to financial markets.
Now, prospect theory is closely
related to a broader concept
called cognitive bias. A
cognitive bias is is a
systematic error in thinking, a
mental shortcut or heuristic
that leads us to make
consistently irrational
judgments. Kahneman later
organized these ideas into what
he called System 1 and System 2.
Thinking, described in his 2011
book Fast and Slow, System 1 is
fast, intuitive and emotional.
System 2 is slow, deliberate and
logical. Most of our everyday
decisions are made by System 1,
which is efficient but prone to
error. Some of the most well
documented cognitive biases
include the availability
heuristic, where we judge the
likelihood of an event based on
how easily an example comes to
mind. After seeing news coverage
of plane crashes, for instance,
people dramatically overestimate
the danger of flying, even
though statistically it is far
safer than driving. Another is
confirmation bias, our tendency
to seek out and interpret
information in ways that confirm
what we already believe while
ignoring contradictory evidence.
The third framework I want to
introduce today is is bounded
rationality, a concept developed
by the economist and cognitive
scientist Herbert Simon in the
1950s. Simon argued that while
people do try to make rational
decisions, we are constrained by
three the limited information
available to us, the cognitive
limitations of our minds, and
the finite amount of time we
have to make a decision. Given
these constraints, we don't
optimize, we satisfice. That's a
word Simon coined, combining,
satisfy and suffice. We choose
the first option that meets an
acceptable threshold rather than
exhaustively comparing all
alternatives. Understanding
these frameworks has significant
real world applications in
public health. Nudge theory,
developed by Thaler and Sunstein
uses knowledge of cognitive bias

guide people toward healthier
choices without restricting
their freedom. For example,
placing fruit at eye level in a
cafeteria increases consumption
not through rules but through
the architecture of choice. In
finance, behavioral economists
have used prospect theory to
explain market bubbles and
crashes, events that classical
economics struggled to account
for. And in legal systems,
awareness of cognitive bias is
reshaping how eyewitness
testimony is evaluated in court.
