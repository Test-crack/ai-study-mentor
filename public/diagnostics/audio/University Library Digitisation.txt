Professor Harris: Before we finalise the first phase of the digitisation project, I want to reconsider how we're choosing which materials to scan first. Leena, your draft gives priority to the oldest items in the archive.

Leena: Age seemed like the most defensible criterion. Some of the manuscripts are more than two hundred years old, so I assumed they were automatically at greatest risk.

Tom: But age and physical vulnerability aren't necessarily the same thing.

Leena: No, that's fair. Some nineteenth-century volumes are remarkably stable, while much more recent newspapers are already becoming brittle.

Professor Harris: Exactly. I don't want us to create a simple oldest-first queue. We need to consider physical condition, demand from researchers, and whether repeated handling is accelerating deterioration.

Leena: In that case, frequently requested fragile items should probably move ahead of older material that's rarely consulted.

Professor Harris: In many cases, yes.

Tom: There's also a technical constraint. The initial proposal assumes we'll scan everything at the highest resolution our equipment allows.

Professor Harris: Wouldn't that give us the best preservation copy?

Tom: Not necessarily in any practical sense. Higher resolution produces larger files, longer processing times, and greater storage requirements. Beyond a certain point, additional detail may have no meaningful benefit for a particular type of material.

Leena: So you're suggesting different specifications for different collections?

Tom: Exactly. A handwritten map with fine annotations may justify a much higher resolution than a clearly printed administrative report.

Professor Harris: Good. But I don't want staff deciding settings arbitrarily from item to item.

Tom: Neither do I. We should establish technical standards by material category and record any exceptions.

Leena: What about damaged documents? Some pages can't be fully flattened without risking further deterioration.

Tom: Then they shouldn't be forced flat simply to produce a cleaner image.

Professor Harris: Agreed. Preservation takes priority over visual perfection.

Leena: That may mean some scans include curvature or shadow near the binding.

Tom: Which is acceptable if correcting it physically would damage the original. Digital processing can compensate for some distortion later, although it shouldn't be used to invent missing text.

Professor Harris: Another issue is access. The proposal says digitised material should be made publicly available immediately after scanning.

Leena: I think that needs qualification. Most material can be released, but some collections contain personal information or are subject to donor restrictions.

Tom: Technically, we can store everything in the repository while limiting public access to selected files.

Professor Harris: So digitisation and publication should be treated as separate decisions.

Leena: Exactly.

Professor Harris: Then our first phase will prioritise vulnerable, heavily used material rather than simply the oldest items; apply scanning standards by material type; avoid physical manipulation that could cause damage; and review access restrictions before publication.

Tom: That gives us a much more defensible workflow.
